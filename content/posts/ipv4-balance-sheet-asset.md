In 2011, IANA — the organisation that manages global internet address allocation — handed out its last IPv4 address block. The free pool was exhausted.

Since then, anyone who needs IPv4 addresses has to buy or lease them from someone who already has them. A secondary market developed. That market is now well-established, with active brokers, registrar-facilitated transfers, and prices that have risen significantly over the past decade.

The businesses that benefit most from this are not the ones actively trading in IP addresses. They are the ones that were allocated address blocks years ago — often through legacy allocations from RIPE or ARIN — and have never thought about them since.

## How businesses end up with unmonetised IPv4 blocks

Legacy technology companies, telcos, universities, and large corporates were allocated address blocks in the early days of the internet when the logic was to allocate generously. A company might have received a /16 block — 65,536 addresses — when it only ever needed a fraction of that.

Acquisitions compound this. When Company A acquires Company B, the IP address blocks from Company B end up on the balance sheet at nominal value. The IT team that actually understands what they are gets absorbed or replaced. Nobody is looking at the RIPE database wondering if there is value sitting there.

## What the market looks like now

IPv4 addresses trade in a range depending on block size, region, and the current supply and demand balance. The market is not perfectly liquid but it is real — there are specialist brokers, established transfer processes through the regional internet registries, and a functioning secondary market.

A /24 block — 256 addresses — might be worth several thousand pounds. Larger blocks scale accordingly. For a business sitting on a /20 or larger, the numbers can be material.

Leasing is also an option for businesses that want to retain their allocation but generate income from addresses they are not using.
